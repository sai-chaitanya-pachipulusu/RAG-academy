## Why this matters in production

If you chunk a document, you'll frequently cut through:

- definitions
- enumerations
- code blocks

Overlap is a cheap mitigation. It increases index size, but often improves recall.

## What you're implementing

Given `chunk_size` and `overlap`:

- step size = `chunk_size - overlap`
- chunk starts: 0, step, 2*step, …

Rules:

- `chunk_size > 0`
- `0 <= overlap < chunk_size`

## Learning Outcomes

After completing this challenge, you will:
- Understand the "boundary loss" problem in document chunking
- Know how to use overlap to preserve context across chunk boundaries
- Be able to trade off index size vs. retrieval quality

## Real-World Impact

| Without Overlap | With Overlap |
|-----------------|--------------|
| "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog" → ["The quick brown", "fox jumps over", "the lazy dog"] | "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog" → ["The quick brown fox", "brown fox jumps over the", "fox jumps over the lazy dog"] |
| Context lost at boundaries | Context preserved across boundaries |

## Production Considerations

**Overlap Trade-offs:**
- **0% overlap**: Smallest index, fastest indexing, potential boundary loss
- **10-20% overlap**: Good balance for most use cases
- **50%+ overlap**: Larger index, diminishing returns

**LangChain Default**: 200 character chunk size with 20 character overlap (10%)

## Common pitfalls

- Allowing `overlap == chunk_size` (infinite loop).
- Using `overlap` as the step size instead of `chunk_size - overlap`.
- Not accounting for overlap in storage cost estimates.

## After you pass

Move to **metadata tagging** and **stable chunk IDs** for production traceability.

---

**💡 Pro Tip:** Overlap is the "secret weapon" that makes simple chunking competitive with fancy semantic chunkers. Many production systems use 10-20% overlap as their default.
